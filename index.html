The knife block is the most expensive way to own an edge

A knife block reads like a purchase: $180 once and the kitchen is set. It is really a subscription that bills on the steel. The blades go dull on a schedule, the block stops cutting in about two years, and a new one takes its place. Fifteen blocks across thirty years is $2,700, which is $90 a year and $900 a decade for the privilege of owning every edge twice, once sharp and once gone.

One good chef knife breaks the cycle. A $150 blade sharpened by a professional four times a year at $12 a visit costs $1,590 across the same thirty years, $53 a year, $530 a decade. Take the sharpening in-house and the number falls off the page: the same knife plus $80 of stones, re-stoned twice over the three decades, totals $390. That is $13 a year, $130 a decade. The spread between the block habit and the stone habit is $2,310, paid for the same slicing, the same dicing, the same dinners.

The usual objection is time, so price it. Fifteen minutes a month at the stones is three hours a year; valued at $25 an hour that is $75, and $13 plus $75 still lands under the block's $90. Per day the comparison is blunter: the block runs about 25 cents a day whether anyone cooks, and the stones cost under 4. The edge never asks which budget funded it. Only the ledger remembers.
